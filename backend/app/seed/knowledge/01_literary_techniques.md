# Literary Techniques for Emotional Poetry

## Imagery
Imagery is language that appeals to the senses — sight, sound, touch, taste, smell. Instead of naming an emotion ("I was sad"), show a concrete picture that carries it ("the tea went cold beside my phone"). Strong emotional poems usually anchor each feeling in one specific, physical detail from the person's story.

## Metaphor
A metaphor says one thing IS another to reveal a hidden likeness: "my smile is a curtain drawn over a storm." Use one controlling metaphor per short poem rather than many mixed ones. Good metaphors come from the user's own world: a student's notebook, a mother's kitchen, a jeepney ride, a phone screen at 2 a.m.

## Simile
A simile compares with "like" or "as": "tired as a candle burned at both ends." Similes are gentler than metaphors and suit simple vocabulary levels.

## Symbolism
A symbol is a concrete object that stands for an idea. Common emotional symbols: dawn and sunrise (hope, new beginnings); dusk and twilight (endings, memory); rain (grief, cleansing); seeds and buds (growth, potential); stars (dreams, guidance); masks and curtains (hidden feelings); bridges (reconciliation); empty chairs (absence, grief); candles and embers (fragile hope).

## Personification
Giving human qualities to non-human things: "the night listens," "the wind remembers your name." It creates intimacy and helps lonely speakers feel accompanied.

## Repetition and Anaphora
Repeating a word or phrase at the start of lines builds emotional intensity: "I give... / I give... / I give, and no one sees." Use for longing, devotion, frustration or prayer-like poems.

## Contrast and Juxtaposition
Placing opposites side by side — smile and tears, crowd and loneliness, light and shadow — dramatizes mixed or conflicting emotions. Ideal when a story contains both happiness and sadness.

## The Turn (Volta)
Many short forms pivot near the end: a shift from problem to resolution, from despair to a small hope, from outer image to inner feeling. Sonnets turn at line 9 (Petrarchan) or the final couplet (Shakespearean); haiku and tanaga often turn in the last line.

## Enjambment and Line Breaks
Enjambment carries a sentence across a line break, creating suspense or a double meaning. End-stopped lines (ending with punctuation) feel calm and complete. In free verse, line breaks are the main rhythm tool.

## Sound Devices
Alliteration (repeated initial consonants: "silent, slow, and still"), assonance (repeated vowel sounds), and consonance add music without needing rhyme. Soft sounds (l, m, s, w) suit sadness and peace; hard sounds (k, t, b, g) suit anger and determination.

## Showing, Not Telling
Name emotions sparingly. Let the reader feel the emotion through action and image. A good test: could a reader guess the emotion even if its name were removed?

## Endings
End on an image or an earned insight, not a moral lecture. When a story is painful, a gentle note of hope can be offered, but never dismiss or rush the person's feelings.
